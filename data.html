Radar measurements of one Rotterdam district, where their zero sits, the heights of its buildings, the balloon, station and tower measurements we compare them with, and the air near the ground rebuilt from those.

Radar: two processings of Kop van Zuid

The radar satellite TerraSAR-X passes over Rotterdam every 11 days. From its images, thousands of fixed points on facades, roof edges and pavement are tracked over the years. We have two processings of the same district, Kop van Zuid, made at different times. They overlap for four years, which lets us check one against the other.

Kop van Zuid is a high-rise best case, not a city average.

Where the zero sits

Every radar movement is measured against some fixed point. Neither data set says which point that was, and the two processings disagree because of it. The team now measures everything against the ground the two share.

Analysis of 29 September 2026. The team adopted the shared ground as its reference on 5 October 2026. Since 13 October ground that itself sinks clearly faster than the rest, such as a subsiding parking lot (about 5.5 mm per year), is left out: 477 of the 499 pairs remain, and the result does not change. A draft paper section on reference choices exists and is not published here.

Why a reference is needed

Radar interferometry never measures absolute positions, only changes, and only relative to a chosen point. If that point itself moves, every other point seems to move the opposite way. For the air signal this matters less than it sounds: the method compares a high point with a low point on the same pass, and anything they share cancels. It matters a great deal as soon as rates, seasons, or the two processings are compared.

Each displacement series is relative to one reference point in space and one reference epoch in time. Neither point set contains its reference point. A common reference signal shifts every series by the same amount per epoch, so it changes rates and seasonal terms, but it leaves a same-epoch height slope with a free intercept unchanged.

The two processings disagree by a constant

Over the four years they share, the same points should move at the same speed in both processings. They do not: the newer one gives every point a rate about 0.6 mm per year higher, whatever its height or position. A constant difference like that is what two different reference points produce.

752 unique mutual-nearest point pairs (coherence above 0.6, within 1.5 m horizontally and 1.5 m in height), linear rates fitted on exactly the same 122 dates: median difference, newer minus older, +0.60 mm/yr, interquartile range 0.38 mm/yr. A plane-plus-height fit to the paired differences has a height coefficient of -0.01 mm/yr per 100 m and leaves the spread unchanged (0.45 mm/yr before and after). The physical cause of the offset is not established by these data.

The ground has a seasonal cycle of its own

The shared-ground series is not perfectly still: in the processor's frame it swings by about half a millimetre over the year, peaking in late winter. That swing enters every point's series once the ground is the reference. It does not change the height slope the air method uses.

For the 499 unique ground pairs, the fitted annual amplitude is 0.498 and 0.506 mm over each full record, and 0.440 and 0.468 mm on the identical dates (older, newer). The earlier 778-entry baseline peaked around day 51 to 52 (20 to 21 February). Subtracting the reference series changed the existing free-intercept height slope by at most 1.09e-14 mm per 100 m, numerical round-off. The amplitude describes a composite reference series; reference motion, atmosphere, local motion and processing effects have not been separated.

Limits of the ground reference

  • "Ground" means radar points less than 5 m above ground with coherence above 0.6. It is a group of low scatterers, not a surveyed benchmark, and the inherited selection includes some points with negative inferred height.
  • It is not a calibrated zero at exactly 3.20 m NAP, the street level the simulation assumes.
  • The original processor reference points are still unknown; what the +0.60 mm/yr is remains a question for the data provider.

Building heights

The method needs to know how high each radar point is, and where the ground is. Heights come from the public 3D building register 3DBAG, which is built from the national elevation model AHN. The pier is flat: the ground sits at about 3.2 m above Dutch sea level (NAP) everywhere.

Area: RD New box (92600, 435200) to (93450, 435950), 46 buildings. Two independent routes give the ground level: the radar product's own elevation model minus its geoid, median 3.20 m NAP with 0.18 m spread over 20 594 pixels, and 3DBAG's ground height, median 3.34 m NAP over 45 buildings. The 0.14 m agreement supports the inferred conversion to NAP and to height above ground. Height model: AHN5 (AHN6 does not yet cover Rotterdam). Roofs in the box reach 158 m; radar points stop at 83 m.

Balloon soundings

Two radiosonde stations frame the region: De Bilt in the Netherlands and Beauvechain in Belgium. Their archived records are the reference for what the air does, but they keep few readings close to the ground. That is why the team reconstructs the lowest 200 m separately; see Air near the ground.

Source: NOAA IGRA2. In 2025 the archive holds 453 De Bilt reports on 358 dates and 331 Beauvechain reports on 329 dates. A large fraction of archived records has no reported height, and most 50 m bins near the surface have a median of zero recorded levels per launch: a property of what the archive retains, not of the flight. In the archive used for the September analyses, the first De Bilt level sits at about 650 m above ground, so any De Bilt value for the lowest 83 m is an interpolation.

Weather stations, towers and a weather model

Ground stations give the temperature on each pass day, two towers give measured temperature at several heights, and a weather model fills in between.

  • KNMI station 344, Rotterdam: daily air temperature, used as the stand-in for building temperature in the estimation tests.
  • Brasschaat tower (ICOS, Belgium): temperature at ten levels from 0.1 to 32.8 m in 2025; the nearby check for Beauvechain.
  • Cabauw tower (KNMI): temperature and humidity from 1.5 to 200 m; the check for De Bilt, for the 2025 months available (January to April, July and August).
  • ICON-D2 weather model, through the Open-Meteo archive: the background the low-level reconstruction starts from.

The air near the ground

Weather balloons keep few readings in the lowest 200 metres, exactly the layer the radar method looks at. So the team rebuilt that layer from what is measured around it, and checked the result against two measurement towers.

Analysis package of 1 October 2026, by Sander da Costa. Year covered: 2025.

Why reconstruct it

The radar compares points between street level and about 80 m. To know what it should see, and to give its measurements an absolute level, we need to know what the air did in that layer. The balloon archives hold the measurements, but keep few of the low readings. The reconstruction combines the readings that are kept, the weather at the ground and a weather model into a full profile every 10 m from the ground to 200 m.

What the model does

It starts from the weather model's forecast for the balloon's time and place, and corrects it towards what was actually measured at the ground and on the balloon. Pressure follows from the weight of the air above; refractivity follows from pressure, temperature and humidity.

A Gaussian model of the discrepancy between the ICON-D2 weather background and observations, conditioned on surface and native sounding temperature and log vapour pressure; hydrostatic pressure; refractivity derived from P, T and e. Stations: De Bilt and Beauvechain, nominal 2025 reports. Output on a 10 m grid from 0 to 200 m, with 2,048 joint draws per profile for uncertainty. Training, tuning and test use different days of each month (days 1 to 14 training, 15 to 20 length validation, 21 to the end test). The 0 to 200 m zenith delay in the output is a partial layer contribution only.

Checked against the Brasschaat tower

What this does not show

  • Brasschaat is a forest site about 65 km from Beauvechain; that the result carries over to Beauvechain, or to a city, is not tested.
  • The tower stops at 32.8 m, so there is no check of the full 200 m from this tower, and none for humidity (public humidity exists at only two heights, the lower sensor in a separate place).
  • Cabauw (1.5 to 200 m, temperature and humidity) is the separate check for De Bilt, but only for the 2025 months available: January to April, July and August.
  • It is a retrospective test on one year, not a forecast for a future year.

Its role in the project

The reconstruction gives the project what the radar cannot supply itself: a realistic picture of the lowest layer, to feed the simulation and to fix the absolute level that radar changes lack. The package already exports profiles in the format the simulation reads. How exactly it enters the rebuilt simulation and the paper is still being worked out.

What may appear on this site

This site is public. The radar point sets were given to us for the course and may not be passed on, so the rule is strict.

  • Everything drawn from the radar data is a mean over at least 20 radar points (a grid cell, a building, a 10 m slab of a building, a height band, the scene), published together with the spread of the points behind it.
  • No radar point, point position, point identifier or single point's time series appears here, in figures or in data files.
  • Fully synthetic tests and figures may appear, marked as synthetic.
  • Public inputs (soundings, weather, towers, buildings) may appear with their licence.
  • The draft text of the paper does not appear until it is submitted.

The exporter in the team repository refuses anything else, and tools/check_data.py in the site repository checks every data file before publication.
