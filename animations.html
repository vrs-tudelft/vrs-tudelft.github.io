Virtual Radiosonde

Animations

Six players, drawn in the browser from small tables of averages. Each moves through the 288 passes, or through one year; the line under each one says which pass you are looking at and what the weather was.

Every colour and every dot is a mean over at least 20 radar points. Play, pause and step with the buttons, drag the slider to scrub, or focus a player and use the arrow keys and the space bar. The players pause when scrolled out of view.

The scene, pass by pass

Kop van Zuid on a 10 m grid, north up; the ground-only grid stays at 25 m because those points are three times sparser. Each cell is coloured by how much its points moved since the previous pass, on a scale that never changes. On most passes the whole pier turns one shade together: that common change is the atmosphere over the whole scene, the reference point, or both. The height-dependent part we are after is the small extra colour on the tall buildings, which is why the trace of s(t) runs underneath.

The height gradient, pass by pass

Above, the mean displacement of ten height bands against their height, relative to the scene mean, with the fitted line for that pass; its slope is s(t). Below, the ten-year series of that slope with a marker following the frame. The tilt above and the curve below are the same quantity, and most of the tilt is seasonal and slow: in summer the high bands sit to the right, in winter to the left.

Taking the gradient apart

The same s(t) in four steps: as measured; the trend removed, the north-east towers sinking faster than the ground; the yearly wave removed, the buildings expanding in summer; and the remainder differenced from pass to pass. That leftover, about 1.7 mm per 100 m, is three times the noise of the fit and about 16 N-units if it were all air. It is a candidate, not a proof.

Buildings or air?

Left, the 11-day changes of s(t), of a lagged air temperature standing in for the buildings, and of the lowest 100 m of refractivity above De Bilt. Right, the change of s(t) against each of the two, one dot per pass filling in as time advances, with the fitted line and, dashed, what physics would give if that explanation carried all of it. Both fitted lines are real and both fall short by the same third. The south-west half is the cleaner test.

The year clock

One year on a clock face, January at the top. As the hand sweeps, each pass drops a dot: the open ground in blue, the buildings above 40 m in red, after offset, trend and curvature are removed per point. The lines are the monthly means; the dotted trace inside is the air temperature. The buildings peak in late July with the air. The open ground peaks in February, half a year away and later than a mirror image would.

A virtual balloon

For every pair of consecutive passes a balloon rises from the lowest height bin to 83 m and drops a dot at each bin: how much that bin's residual changed over the 11 days, relative to the lowest bin. The red line is the fit through the dots and its slope is the layer gradient, the one number per pass the project retrieves today. The dashed line is what the De Bilt sounding predicts for the same 11 days. On most passes the dots fall close to a straight line, and the two series on the right follow each other with a correlation of about 0.56.
