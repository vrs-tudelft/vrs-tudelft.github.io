Virtual Radiosonde

Animations

Six players, drawn in the browser from small tables of averages. Each one moves through the 288 satellite passes, or through one year, and the label under it tells you which pass you are looking at and what the weather was.

Every colour and every dot is a mean over at least 20 radar points: a 25 m cell, a height band, a group of buildings. No single measurement point is on this site. Use the buttons to play, pause and step, drag the slider to scrub, or focus a player and use the arrow keys and space bar. The players pause when scrolled out of view.

On this page: The scene, pass by pass The height gradient Taking it apart Buildings or air The year clock A virtual balloon

1. The scene, pass by pass

Kop van Zuid on a 25 m grid, north up, one frame per pass. Each cell is coloured by how much its radar points moved since the previous pass, 11 days earlier. The colour scale is fixed for all frames and symmetric around zero, so a calm pass looks calm and an eventful one colours the whole peninsula at once. The slow sinking of the north-east end is differenced away in this view; switch to "displacement since 2014" to watch it build up instead, or to "minus each cell's trend" to see the yearly breathing without the sinking.

What to look for: on most passes the whole scene turns one shade of red or blue together. That common change is the atmosphere, the reference point, or both, and it is the same everywhere. The height-dependent part we are after is the small extra colour on the tall buildings, which is why the animation is paired with the trace of s(t) underneath.

This animation needs JavaScript.

Data behind it: data/cells.json and data/cells.bin (112 cells with all points, 62 with ground points, each at least 20 points; mean displacement per cell per pass), data/outlines.json (BAG footprints, CC0), data/epochs.json. Static counterparts: figures 01, 06 and 25 on the Findings and Buildings and ground pages.

2. The height gradient, pass by pass

Top: the mean displacement of ten height bands, relative to the scene mean, against the height of the band. The red line is the weighted fit through all points for that pass; its slope is the height gradient s(t), in millimetres per 100 m of height. Bottom: the full ten-year series of that slope, with a marker following the frame. The tilt above and the curve below are the same quantity.

What to look for: the tilt is mostly seasonal and slow. In summer the high bands sit to the right (the buildings have expanded towards the satellite), in winter to the left. The fast part, the thing that changes from one frame to the next, is small next to that swing, which is why the next animation takes the slow parts out.

This animation needs JavaScript.

Data behind it: data/bands.json (ten bands of 119 to 3 273 points), data/epochs.json (the slope per pass, coherence-weighted, 11 818 points). Static counterparts: figures 06, 07 and 24.

3. Taking the gradient apart

The same s(t) in four steps. First as measured. Then the fitted trend is removed: the north-east towers sinking faster than the ground, which tilts the whole series. Then the fitted yearly wave: the buildings expanding in summer and shrinking in winter, the largest term of all. What is left is differenced from pass to pass. That leftover, about 1.7 mm per 100 m from one pass to the next, is three times the formal noise of the fit and corresponds to about 16 N-units of refractivity if it were all air.

What to look for: how much of s(t) is explained before the atmosphere is even considered, and how the leftover is a candidate, not a proof. Any building motion that varies with height and is not a trend or a yearly wave would produce the same number.

This animation needs JavaScript.

Data behind it: data/epochs.json (s(t), its fitted model, the residual and the 11-day changes). Static counterpart: figure 08.

4. Buildings or air?

Two things could make the height gradient change between passes: the buildings changing temperature, and the air changing refractivity. Left: the 11-day change of s(t), the change of a lagged Rotterdam air temperature (a stand-in for the building temperature), and the change of the lowest 100 m of refractivity from the De Bilt balloon. Right: the change of s(t) against each of the two, one dot per pass, filling in as time advances, with the fitted line and, dashed, the line physics would give if that explanation carried all of it.

What to look for: both fitted lines are real and both fall short of the dashed physics line, at 60 to 70 % of the predicted size. The two explanations are barely tangled (variance inflation 1.12), so one geometry can tell them apart at the 11-day scale. Switch to the south-west half, which has no sinking towers, for the cleaner test.

This animation needs JavaScript.

Data behind it: data/joint.json (287 passes, scene-wide series and the fit), data/epochs.json. Static counterparts: figures 14 and 15.

5. The year clock

One year on a clock face, January at the top. As the hand sweeps, each pass drops a dot at its day of the year: the mean displacement of the open ground in blue, of the buildings above 40 m in red, after each point's offset, trend and curvature are removed. The lines are the monthly means. The dotted grey trace inside is the air temperature on its own scale.

What to look for: the buildings peak in late July with the air. The open ground peaks in February, half a year away, and two to three weeks later than a plain mirror image of the buildings would (day 28). The ground at the towers' feet, off by default, shows almost no cycle. Why the ground moves in winter is an open question; the candidates are the reference point of the radar processing, the near-surface refractivity cycle, and real motion of the fill.

This animation needs JavaScript.

Data behind it: data/yearclock.json (groups of 1 505 to 2 707 points, the KNMI monthly means, the De Bilt layer refractivity). Static counterparts: figures 21 and 27.

6. A virtual balloon

For every pair of consecutive passes, a balloon rises from the lowest height bin to 83 m. At each bin it drops a dot: how much that bin's residual changed over the 11 days, relative to the lowest bin, with its standard error. The red line is the straight-line fit through the dots; its slope is the layer gradient, the one number per pass the project retrieves today, and the label converts it to N-units. The dashed line is what the De Bilt sounding, interpolated across the same layer, predicts for the same 11 days. Right: both series over the decade.

What to look for: the dots fall close to a straight line on most passes. A line explains about two thirds of the binned profile and a parabola only a little more, so at this sensor's precision the retrieval is a mean layer value, not a resolved profile. The De Bilt prediction has the right size (1.18 predicted against 1.69 measured, rms) and follows the radar pass by pass with a correlation of about 0.56, from a balloon launched at midnight 50 km away.

This animation needs JavaScript.

Data behind it: data/profile.json (ten bins of 119 to 3 273 points; per-point offset, trend and annual term removed; De Bilt 00 UTC soundings within 36 h of the pass), data/epochs.json. Static counterparts: figures 17, 19 and 28.

All six players are drawn from the same small set of aggregate files, listed on the data page with the rule they obey. The first two animations replaced the matplotlib frame players of September 3, which carried the same story as still images.
