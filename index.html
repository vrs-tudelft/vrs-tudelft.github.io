Virtual Radiosonde

A weather balloon that never launches

We are trying to read the lowest hundred metres of the air above a city from satellite radar, using the buildings themselves as the measuring stick.

A radiosonde is the instrument on a weather balloon. It measures pressure, temperature and humidity all the way up. It is the reference measurement of the atmosphere, and it is rare. The Netherlands launches one a day, from De Bilt, around midnight, and never over a city. Cities are where the air near the ground is most disturbed and where knowing it matters most: heat, air quality, weather models.

Radar satellites see thousands of fixed points on building facades, and interferometry measures their motion to a millimetre. The air the signal travels through delays it, and a point high on a tower looks through less of the lowest air than a point near the street. Take the difference between the two, and again between one pass and the first, and only the layer between the two heights is left. Everything above both points cancels. In the geodetic use of radar this height-dependent delay is an error that gets removed. Here it is the product.

That last clause is the whole difficulty. Buildings sink, and the newest towers on the pier still sink at 4 mm a year. Buildings grow when warm and shrink when cold, and a tall building grows more, so thermal expansion looks exactly like a height-dependent air signal. A warm day thins the air, which shortens the delay to the high point by about 0.1 mm per kelvin per 100 m, and warms the building, which lengthens it by about 1 mm. Same driver, opposite sign, and the building term is eight times larger. The two have to be fitted together, and the findings show how far that has come.

What comes out is not a temperature profile and not a forecast. It is the refractivity of a layer of air, one number for humidity and temperature together, once every 11 days, over districts with tall buildings, for as long as the satellite keeps passing. Ten years so far.

Five numbers

The first data set covers Kop van Zuid in Rotterdam: 20 594 radar points, observed 288 times over ten years by TerraSAR-X. Everything else builds on these.

What we have found so far

  • There is a height-dependent signal on 97 % of the passes. Most of it is the buildings: a slow tilt from the north-east towers sinking, and a yearly wave from thermal expansion.
  • What is left is scene-wide and truly about height. Two halves of the scene agree on it (correlation 0.92), and pairs of points 16 m apart give 82 % of the scene-wide value.
  • Buildings and air can be told apart, and each explains part of it: temperature at 0.62 and refractivity at 0.064 mm per 100 m, against 0.92 and 0.109 predicted.
  • The balloon 50 km away agrees on size and, pass by pass, on timing. De Bilt predicts 1.18 mm per 100 m of 11-day change, we measure 1.69, and the two correlate at 0.56 across 287 steps.
  • The retrieval is one number per pass, not yet a profile. A straight line explains two thirds of the binned height differences; a curve adds little.
  • The ground is not a clean zero. The open quays breathe with the seasons in the opposite phase to the buildings, by 0.5 to 0.8 mm, peaking in February. Why is open.

About

A student team project of the course Applied Space Geodesy at Delft University of Technology, 31 August to 22 October 2026, supervised by Ramon Hanssen with Freek van Leijen (TU Delft) and Hanno Maljaars (SkyGeo). Twelve MSc students from Civil Engineering and Geosciences, Aerospace Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, Geomatics and Computer Science: Sander da Costa, Jaap de Ruiter, Dirk van Dale, Alejandro Giner Gil, Phoebe Smirk, Quinten Uivel, Lars van Dijk, Rosalyn van Hemert, Shaquille Huiswoud, Akhil Veeranki, Jihun Yoo and Oula Zhu. The paper is written by the whole team and is not on this site.

The analysis code, the figures and this site were made by Quinten Uivel, with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) for the repositories, the plotting libraries, the site and the drafting of captions from the numbers the scripts produced; no number was invented, every figure regenerates from a script in the team repository, and every interpretation here is a hypothesis and says so. The ground-minus-roof check against surface weather is Jaap de Ruiter's; the radiosonde work is Sander da Costa's; the data set, the idea and the questions that shaped the findings are Ramon Hanssen's.

The TerraSAR-X point set was provided for the course and may not be passed on. No radar point, position or single time series is on this site: everything drawn from the radar data is a mean over at least 20 points, and a script checks every file against that rule.
