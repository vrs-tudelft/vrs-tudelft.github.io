Virtual Radiosonde

A weather balloon that never launches

We are trying to read the lowest hundred metres of the air above a city from satellite radar, using the buildings themselves as the measuring stick.

A radiosonde measures pressure, temperature and humidity all the way up. It is the reference measurement of the atmosphere, and it is rare. The Netherlands launches one a day, from De Bilt, around midnight, and never over a city. Cities are where the air near the ground is most disturbed and where knowing it matters most: heat, air quality, weather models.

Radar satellites see thousands of fixed points on building facades, and interferometry measures their motion to a millimetre. The air the signal travels through delays it, and a point high on a tower looks through less of the lowest air than a point near the street. Take the difference between the two, and again between one pass and the first, and only the layer between the two heights is left. Everything above both points cancels. In the geodetic use of radar this height-dependent delay is an error that gets removed. Here it is the product.

That last clause is the whole difficulty. Buildings sink, and the newest towers on the pier still sink at 4 mm a year. Buildings grow when warm and shrink when cold, and a tall building grows more, so thermal expansion looks exactly like a height-dependent air signal. The two have to be fitted together, and the findings show how far that has come.

What comes out is not a temperature profile and not a forecast. It is the refractivity of a layer of air, one number for humidity and temperature together, once every 11 days, over districts with tall buildings, for as long as the satellite keeps passing. Ten years so far.

What we have found

  • There is a height-dependent signal on 97 % of the passes. Most of it is the buildings: a slow tilt from the north-east towers sinking, and a yearly wave from thermal expansion.
  • What is left is scene-wide and truly about height. Two halves of the scene agree on it, and neighbouring points agree too.
  • Buildings and air can be told apart: temperature at 0.62 and refractivity at 0.064 mm per 100 m, against 0.92 and 0.109 predicted.
  • The balloon agrees on size and, pass by pass, on timing: it predicts 1.18 mm per 100 m of 11-day change where we measure 1.69.
  • The retrieval is one number per pass, not yet a profile. A straight line explains two thirds of the binned height differences; a curve adds little.
  • The ground is not a clean zero. The open quays breathe with the seasons in the opposite phase to the buildings, by 0.5 to 0.8 mm, peaking in February. Why is open.

Who made what is on this site

The analysis code, the figures and this site: Quinten Uivel, with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) for the repositories, the plotting libraries, the site itself and the drafting of captions from the numbers the scripts produced. No number was invented; every figure regenerates from a script in the team repository, and every interpretation here is a hypothesis and says so. The ground-minus-roof check against surface weather is Jaap de Ruiter's; the radiosonde work is Sander da Costa's; the data set, the idea and the questions that shaped the findings are Ramon Hanssen's.

What is on this site, and what is not

The TerraSAR-X point set was provided for the course and may not be passed on. No radar point, position or single time series is here. Everything drawn from the radar data is a mean over at least 20 points: a cell on a fixed grid of 10 m, or 25 m for ground points only, a building, a 10 m slab of a building, a height band, or the whole scene. A script checks every published file against that rule. The draft text of the paper is not here either.
