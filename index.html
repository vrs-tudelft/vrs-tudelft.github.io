Virtual Radiosonde

A weather balloon that never launches

We are trying to read the lowest hundred metres of the air above a city from satellite radar, using the buildings themselves as the measuring stick.

A radiosonde is the instrument on a weather balloon. It measures pressure, temperature and humidity all the way up. It is the reference measurement of the atmosphere, and it is rare: the Netherlands launches from one place, De Bilt, once a day, and never over a city. Cities are where the air near the ground is most disturbed and where knowing it matters most.

Radar satellites see thousands of fixed points on building facades. A point high on a tower and a point near the street look through different amounts of air on the way to the satellite. The difference depends on how humid and how warm that layer of air is. If we can separate that small effect from everything else that moves a building, we get a reading of the air between the two heights, every time the satellite passes.

This is a student team project for the course Applied Space Geodesy at TU Delft (September to October 2026), supervised by Prof. Ramon Hanssen. Everything here is work in progress. Nothing on this site is a validated result unless it says so, and every number comes with its catch.

Five numbers

Our first data set covers Kop van Zuid in Rotterdam: 20 594 radar points, observed 288 times over ten years by the TerraSAR-X satellite. These are the numbers everything else builds on.

Time between passes11 daysVerified from the 288 dates. A long-term record, never an hourly or real-time product. The hour of the pass is not in the file; from the orbit it is probably early morning.

What we have found so far

  • There is a height-dependent signal on almost every pass (97 % of them), and most of it is the buildings: a slow tilt from the north-east towers sinking, and a yearly wave from thermal expansion. Findings A.
  • What is left after those two is scene-wide and truly about height. Two independent halves of the scene agree on it (correlation 0.92), and pairs of points 16 m apart give 82 % of the scene-wide value. Findings B.
  • Buildings and air can be told apart, and each explains part of it. Temperature at 0.62 and refractivity at 0.064 mm per 100 m, against 0.92 and 0.109 predicted. Findings C.
  • The balloon 50 km away agrees on size and, pass by pass, on timing. De Bilt predicts 1.18 mm per 100 m of 11-day change, we measure 1.69, and the two correlate at 0.56 across 287 steps. Findings C, the virtual balloon.
  • The retrieval is one number per pass, not yet a profile. A straight line explains two thirds of the binned height differences; a curve adds little. Findings D.
  • The ground is not a clean zero. The open quays breathe with the seasons in the opposite phase to the buildings, 0.5 to 0.8 mm peaking in February, and the newest towers still sink at 4 mm per year. Buildings and ground.
