Virtual Radiosonde

The idea

Five questions, in the order the course asked them on its first day: what is a radiosonde, what is satellite radar interferometry, how are they related, what is the idea, and why do this.

On this page: What a radiosonde measures What radar measures How they are related The idea The competitor Why do it What it is not

1. What a radiosonde measures

A radiosonde is a small instrument package carried up by a weather balloon. It reports six things on the way: pressure, temperature, relative humidity, its position from satellite navigation (and from that its height), wind speed and direction from how the position changes, and the time of each reading. It rises at about 5 m per second, so the lowest 200 m are gone in forty seconds, while the sensors are still settling. The balloon bursts at 17 to 25 km and the instrument is not recovered.

It is the reference measurement of the atmosphere and it is scarce. The Netherlands launches from one place, De Bilt, once a day around midnight, 50 km from Rotterdam and never over a city. Worse for us, the public archive we use keeps only the standard levels: the first reading above the ground is typically 650 m up, so everything it says about the lowest 100 m is a straight line drawn between the surface and that level. The one fixed alternative is the Cabauw tower, 213 m high with instruments at 2, 10, 20, 40, 80, 120 and 200 m, also in the countryside.

Catch. The De Bilt sounding is our ground truth and our simulation input, but it cannot validate the shape of a profile below 100 m, only its mean gradient. The KNMI high-resolution files, with a reading every few metres, exist and are the next thing to get.

2. What satellite radar interferometry measures

A radar satellite sends microwave pulses to the ground and records the echoes. Comparing the phase of the echoes from two passes, interferometry, gives the change in the distance between the satellite and each reflecting point to a fraction of the wavelength. TerraSAR-X works at 3.1 cm, so one full phase cycle is 1.55 cm of two-way path, and with many passes the motion of a stable reflector comes out at millimetre precision.

That distance change is a sum. It contains the topography (removed with a height model), the real displacement of the reflector, and the delay of the signal in the air it travelled through. In the geodetic use of radar, the air is noise; whole chapters of the supervisor's 2001 textbook are about removing it, and the part that varies with height is derived there as an error. This project turns that error into the product.

In a city the stable reflectors are pieces of buildings: window ledges, balconies, roof edges, all facing the satellite. Kop van Zuid has 20 594 of them in our set, at heights from the pavement to 83 m.

3. How they are related

The radiosonde's three thermodynamic readings, pressure, temperature and humidity, together fix the refractive index of the air: how much slower than in vacuum a radio wave travels. Refractivity N is that index minus one, times a million, a number around 300 near the ground. Humidity carries most of the variation, temperature next, pressure changes slowly and predictably with height. So the refractivity along the path of the radar signal sets its delay, and knowing the delay tells you the integrated refractivity, which is where the balloon's readings come back in.

The numbers on the arrows matter. A hundred metres of ordinary air near the ground (15 °C, half saturated, N about 310) delays the signal by 31 mm. The whole atmosphere, if you look straight up, delays it by about 2.3 m of which less than 0.3 m is water vapour. Interferometry is not sensitive to those totals, only to how they differ between two points and two passes, and that difference is what a city's height range lets us take.

4. The idea: two heights, one layer of air

A single ray gives an integrated column, not a profile, and it does so only as a difference in time and relative to a reference in space. The vertical information has to come from somewhere else, and in a city it comes from the buildings: reflectors at different heights on the facades see different lengths of the near-surface air. Take the difference between a high and a low reflector, and again between one pass and the first, and the layer between the two heights is the only air that survives. Repeat that over thousands of points and 288 passes and the height gradient of the delay, one number per pass, is a reading of the refractivity of the lowest layer. Stack several height levels and, in principle, it becomes a profile.

This is why the project is called a virtual radiosonde: the city stands in for the balloon, and the satellite passing overhead every 11 days reads it.

The competitor: the buildings move too

The catch is in the last clause of the drawing. Buildings sink, and the newest towers on the pier still sink at 4 mm a year. Buildings expand when warm and shrink when cold, and a tall building expands more, so thermal expansion looks exactly like a height-dependent air signal. The two even have the same driver: a warm day thins the air, which shortens the delay to the high point, and warms the building, which lengthens it. Opposite signs, same height dependence, and the building term is about eight times larger per degree.

In the data the yearly wave of the height gradient is 5.3 mm per 100 m, three times the pass-to-pass signal we are after, and the buildings follow about half to two thirds of the air temperature's swing. Separating the two is the whole difficulty of the project, and the findings page shows how far that has come.

5. Why do this

Urban meteorology is measured at or near the surface, while the processes that matter, heat, pollution, ventilation, depend on the vertical structure of the lowest few hundred metres. The paper's rationale names five uses for repeated, spatially dense profiles of that layer:

  • The urban heat island. How high does the city's extra warmth reach at night, and does a shallow inversion trap it? Rotterdam's downtown was on average 2.3 K warmer than the countryside on 233 summer nights measured in 2010 to 2012, and 6.2 K or more on the hottest one in twenty. Nobody knows how far up that goes, and the humidity half of heat stress is measured even less.
  • Air quality. Whether pollutants mix upward or stay in a thin layer depends on the stability of exactly this layer.
  • The height of the mixed layer and the roughness sublayer above the roofs, which weather and dispersion models parameterise rather than observe.
  • Extreme heat, and whether night-time cooling is confined to a shallow layer above the streets.
  • Weather models. The Dutch forecast model still treats a city as a single tile, because there is no dense urban observation to calibrate anything better. GNSS meteorology showed the way a generation ago, turning the delay of navigation signals into water-vapour columns that forecasters now assimilate; radar over a city would be the same idea, many points deep, with height.

The satellite's orbit adds a twist we did not choose. TerraSAR-X flies a dawn-dusk orbit, so the descending pass over Rotterdam is in the early morning, around the end of the night-time heat island. That is our own reading of the orbit, not a confirmed hour, and confirming it is one of the open questions.

What it is not

  • Not a warning system. One reading every 11 days is a long-term record, not a forecast tool.
  • Not hourly. The satellite passes at one fixed time of day.
  • Not city-wide in the same quality. It needs tall buildings with reflectors at many heights; low-rise districts are served worst.
  • Not a temperature profile. What comes out is a refractivity gradient of a layer: humidity and temperature mixed, pressure predictable. Reading it as temperature or humidity separately needs another measurement.
  • Not an absolute delay. Everything is relative to a reference point and to the first pass; what the first pass looked like is reconstructed statistically from the stack.
