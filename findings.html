Findings

The plots, grouped by the question they answer rather than by the day we made them. Each one says what you see, what it tells us, and why it matters. Heights are metres above ground unless a plot says otherwise; "LOS" is the line of sight from the satellite; s(t) is the height gradient, how much the displacement changes per 100 m of height on one pass.

A. Is there a height-dependent signal at all?

Yes, on 97 % of the passes, and most of it is the buildings. Fit a straight line of displacement against height across all points on one pass; the slope is s(t). It has a slow trend, a large yearly wave, and a pass-to-pass remainder of 1.7 mm per 100 m that is three times the noise of the fit.

B. Is the fast signal scene-wide, and truly about height?

Both. Independent halves of the scene see the same pass-to-pass changes, and pairs of points a few tens of metres apart give almost the same gradient as the whole scene, so a horizontal gradient is not masquerading as a vertical one.

C. Buildings or air?

Both, and they can be told apart. Fit the 11-day changes of s(t) on two things at once: the change of a lagged air temperature, standing in for the temperature of the buildings, and the change of the lowest 100 m of refractivity from the De Bilt balloon. Each explains part of it, the two are barely tangled, and both come out at 60 to 70 % of what physics predicts.

D. Towards a profile, and the ground-truth problem

Today the retrieval is one number per pass. Group the points by height and the 11-day changes fall on a straight line; a curve adds little. And the balloon archive cannot check a profile shape below 650 m anyway.

Figure 19, the De Bilt reference, is on the data page: the sonde's first level is about 650 m up, the pressure part of the layer is predictable (−2.3 ± 0.4 N-units over 0 to 83 m) and the wet part is the signal (−1.2 ± 1.0).

E. An independent check from the ground up

A teammate took a different route: the mean of the ground points (below 5 m) minus the mean of the roof points (55 to 70 m above sea level), 58 m apart, against the wet delay computed from the KNMI surface weather. After trend and season are removed, the two agree with a regression slope of 1.08 ± 0.11 against a predicted 1: the surface-weather forward model is unbiased in amplitude, not merely correlated. The same analysis puts the atmosphere at about 0.7 mm of the 3.0 mm yearly wave in that difference, the buildings at about 2.1 mm, peaking eleven days apart, too close to separate by timing alone. The pass-to-pass band is where they separate.
