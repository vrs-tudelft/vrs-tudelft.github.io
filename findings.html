Is there a height-dependent signal at all?

Yes, on 97 % of the passes, and most of it is the buildings. Fit a straight line of displacement against height across all points on one pass; the slope is s(t). It has a slow trend of −2.56 mm per 100 m per year, the north-east towers sinking faster than the ground, and a yearly wave of 5.26 mm per 100 m, the buildings expanding in summer. What changes from one pass to the next after those two are removed is 1.69 mm per 100 m, three times the formal noise of the fit and about 16 N-units of refractivity if it were all air. That number is the candidate air signal, and any building motion that varies with height and is neither a trend nor a yearly wave would produce it too.

Is the fast signal scene-wide, and truly about height?

Both. Independent halves of the scene see the same pass-to-pass changes (correlation 0.92 split along the pier, 0.91 split east to west), while the slow sinking sits in the north-east half only. And pairs of points a few tens of metres apart give almost the same gradient as the whole scene: 82 % of it at 16 m separation, with a correlation of 0.95, rising to the full value at 250 m. A horizontal gradient is not masquerading as a vertical one.

Buildings or air?

Both, and they can be told apart. Fit the 11-day changes of s(t) on two things at once: the change of a lagged air temperature, standing in for the temperature of the buildings, and the change of the lowest 100 m of refractivity from the De Bilt balloon. Temperature comes out at 0.62 ± 0.04 mm per 100 m per kelvin against 0.92 predicted; refractivity at 0.064 ± 0.006 mm per 100 m per N-unit against 0.109. The two are barely tangled (variance inflation 1.12), the buildings want a lag of about five days, and both fall short of physics by the same third. Why is open: buildings that are not free to expand, a balloon 50 km away, the unknown pass hour, or an atmospheric filter in the radar processing.

On the 49 pass days with both a midnight and a noon sounding, the noon one explains the radar no better (figure 16). Time of day is not the gap between balloon and radar; distance probably is.

Towards a profile

Today the retrieval is one number per pass. Group the points into ten height bins and the 11-day changes fall on a straight line: a line explains 65 % of the binned variance, a parabola 78 %, and the departure from the line has a fixed shape, a kink around 20 to 30 m, the same on every pass. At this sensor's precision and this city's height range the product is a mean layer value. The balloon archive could not check a profile shape anyway: its first level is about 650 m up, so every De Bilt number for our layer is an interpolation. The pressure part of that layer is predictable (−2.3 ± 0.4 N-units over 0 to 83 m); the humidity part is the signal (−1.2 ± 1.0).

An independent check took a different route: the mean of the ground points minus the mean of the roof points, 58 m apart, against the wet delay computed from surface weather. After trend and season are removed the two agree with a regression slope of 1.08 ± 0.11 against a predicted 1. The atmosphere is about 0.7 mm of the 3.0 mm yearly wave in that difference, the buildings about 2.1 mm, and the two peak eleven days apart, too close to separate by timing alone. The pass-to-pass band is where they separate.

The ground

One ground point in figure 2 moves with the seasons, peaking in winter, opposite to the buildings. Is that one point? No. All 3 369 ground points below 5 m were grouped by their distance to the nearest tall building. The open ground as a whole moves towards the satellite in winter and away in summer, by 0.5 to 0.8 mm, peaking in mid-February; the 1 500 points within 30 m of a tower show almost no cycle (0.1 mm). Per point, 48 % peak between November and February and 30 % between May and September.

The cause is open, and three candidates remain. The reference point: every displacement is relative to one reflector, and if that sits on a building that expands in summer, every other point inherits the mirror image. Against it, a pure mirror would peak around day 28, the open ground peaks on day 45, and the towers' feet show nothing. The near-surface refractivity cycle: the lowest 100 m above De Bilt are 11 N-units more refractive in August than in February, which gives a ground point 1.2 mm of seasonal delay per 100 m of height difference to the reference point, in exactly this phase. Real motion of the fill: the pier is fill over clay and peat, it sinks at 2.5 to 4 mm a year next to buildings that do not, and groundwater peaks in February. The ascending track would separate the first two from the third; the location and height of the reference point would settle most of it.

Our reading: the spatial pattern argues hardest against a reference point on an expanding building, because a mirror image would be the same everywhere and it is not. Whether the fill breathes or the air does is the question we most want to put to the ascending track.

Where does the ground sink? In areas, not scattered. The quartiles of the ground rate are −3.6, −1.2 and −0.6 mm per year; the south-west tip around the 1900 hotel is stable, the middle of the pier sinks at 1 to 3.5 mm a year, the north-east end around the 2013 and 2017 towers at 6 to 7. In 50 m cells the spread between cells is twice the spread inside them. Next to every building the pavement sinks at 2.5 to 4 mm a year whatever the building's age. The cause is not in the data: the age of the fill, the loads of the new towers and their underground garages are all candidates.

Our reading: the north-east end is the newest fill carrying the newest and heaviest towers; that the pavement sinks faster than towers standing on piles is what one expects if the fill is still consolidating.

The buildings

Building by building, joined to the outlines of the national register with their construction year. The newer the tower, the faster it still sinks and the more it is slowing down: structure rates of −0.9, −1.2, −2.3, −3.8 and −3.7 mm per year for buildings from 2000, 2005, 2010, 2013 and 2017, with curvatures rising from +0.02 to +0.24 mm per year squared. Hotel New York, from 1900, has stopped: 196 points at −0.6 mm per year with no curvature, while the pavement around it sinks at −2.5. So it is the ground, not the roads: only the two newest towers sink about as fast as their surroundings. Within a building the rate changes with height, by −0.7 to −4.9 mm per 100 m per year, largest for the youngest concrete; shrinkage is the plausible reading, and a height error leaking into the velocity in the radar processing the alternative only its producer can weigh.

Our reading: the two newest towers sinking with their surroundings and slowing down looks like consolidation under load; the tilt on top of it, largest for the youngest concrete, fits shrinkage. Names other than De Rotterdam and Hotel New York are our guess from the aerial image.

Do the buildings expand sideways too? Seen from the east-south-east, the satellite sees 0.385 of any eastward motion, so a structure that expands about a fixed point should show a seasonal term that grows with easting, at +0.015 mm per metre for a 4 K swing. Inside buildings it does, at +0.011 mm per metre, positive in 74 % of the 25 m windows and for all eight buildings with most points: about 70 % of free expansion. The vertical term inside buildings is +0.037 mm per metre, a 4 K swing against 7 K in the air. On the open ground the slopes have both signs. The ascending track would flip this term and nothing else, which makes it a clean test.

Our reading: seventy per cent of free expansion is what one expects of a structure tied to its foundation and its neighbours, and it explains part of why two points seven metres apart on one facade swing less differently than pure vertical expansion predicts. The ascending stack is worth asking for now.

For the retrieval this means a nuisance model per building, rate = a + b·height and seasonal term = c + d·height + e·easting, and a lowest bin that carries an unknown seasonal term until the reference point is known.
