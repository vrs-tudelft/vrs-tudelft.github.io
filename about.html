Virtual Radiosonde

About

A student team project of the course Applied Space Geodesy (AESM5240C) at Delft University of Technology, 31 August to 22 October 2026.

The course

The course takes an idea through research to a scientific publication and towards a minimum viable product. The 2026 idea is the supervisor's: create a virtual radiosonde for the urban environment using satellite radar interferometry. The team meets three times a week, writes one paper together, and presents it on 22 October 2026, with a midterm in the week of 21 September.

Twelve MSc students from Civil Engineering and Geosciences, Aerospace Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, Geomatics and Computer Science, in alphabetical order as on the paper: Sander da Costa, Jaap de Ruiter, Dirk van Dale, Alejandro Giner Gil, Phoebe Smirk, Quinten Uivel, Lars van Dijk, Rosalyn van Hemert, Shaquille Huiswoud, Akhil Veeranki, Jihun Yoo and Oula Zhu. Reproducibility

Every figure is made by a script in the team repository (mvp/src/ of virtual-radiosonde) and has a dated note in research/notes/ there that names the script, the data and every number with its caveat. The captions here are the plain-language version of those notes. The aggregate files behind the animations and the 3D view are written by site_export.py and site_3d_export.py in the same folder, and checked by tools/check_data.py in the site repository. The site is plain HTML with no build step.

The data rule

The TerraSAR-X point set was provided for the course and may not be passed on. No radar point, position or single time series is on this site. Everything drawn from the radar data is a mean over at least 20 points: a 25 m cell on a fixed grid, a building, a 10 m slab of a building, a height band or the whole scene. The positions that appear are those of public things: building outlines and grid cells. How it works.

The use of an AI assistant

An AI coding assistant (Claude Code) was used to build this site, its animations and its 3D view, to set up the team's repositories, to choose and wire the Python libraries behind the figures, and to draft captions and notes from the numbers the scripts produced. The inputs were the point set, the KNMI and IGRA2 records and the open building registers named above. No number was invented: every figure regenerates from the scripts, and the notes record what was computed, how, and what it does not show. The analyses were directed and checked by the team member named above; the interpretations on this site are hypotheses and say so. The paper carries its own statement.
