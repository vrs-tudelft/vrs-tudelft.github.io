The success ladder

The chain and its equations

The refractivity of moist air, with pressure and vapour pressure in hectopascal and temperature in kelvin:

N = 77.6 pd/T + 72 e/T + 3.75·105 e/T2

The first term is the dry, hydrostatic part; the last two are the wet part. The delay along the ray is the integral of N, and the phase follows from the wavelength:

d = 10−6 ∫ N ds   ψ = 4π d / λ   slant = zenith / cos 23°

For orientation: looking straight up through the whole atmosphere the hydrostatic delay is about 2.3 m and the wet delay less than 0.3 m; a hundred metres of ordinary air near the ground is 31 mm. Interferometry sees none of those totals, only differences between points and between passes. The vapour pressure comes from the relative humidity through the Magnus formula, e = RH/100 · es(T) with es(T) = 6.112 exp(17.62 T / (243.12 + T)), T in degrees Celsius.

The competitor, in the same units: a temperature change of one kelvin lowers the refractivity by about one N-unit, which shortens the delay to a point 100 m up by about 0.12 mm, and lengthens a 100 m building by about 1 mm (expansion coefficient 10 per million per kelvin). Same driver, opposite sign, the building term eight times larger. The idea page draws it.

The double difference

The spatial difference removes the reference point, the orbit and the air above both points; the temporal difference removes the topography and any constant. What survives is the change, between two passes, of the delay through the layer between the two heights, plus the change of the buildings themselves. The reference pass is not special physically, only in the processing, and its atmospheric state can be recovered from the stack: with m passes all differenced against the first, the sum of the interferograms equals the sum of the true states minus m times the first state, and the sum of the true states averages to a known climatology. Every "absolute" profile therefore rests on that statistical step.

The height-difference inversion

With height levels at fixed steps and one refractivity per layer, the delay difference between a level and the reference level is the sum of the layers crossed, so the system is solved from the bottom up, one new unknown per row. In the data so far the profile is a straight line with a fixed kink, so the honest product is one layer mean per pass; the six-layer version waits for a second track, more points, or a sharper sensor.

The end-to-end simulation

Before the inverse is trusted on real data, the forward model is run on known inputs. The plan: take the real heights of the scene (the national height model and the 3D building register, see three heights), simulate profiles of pressure, temperature and humidity (random within physical bounds, then realistic, then real soundings), convert to refractivity and to the delay at each height with the reference point as zero, difference two passes, convert to phase and project to the line of sight. Then add what the real data has: noise, displacement, the cross-range position of a reflector, thermal expansion. The questions it must answer: how many points are needed, at which heights, for which precision of the layer refractivity. It has not been run yet; the height input is prepared (figure 30).

How residuals are made today

The simplest defensible way, applied to every point with coherence above 0.6: fit an offset, a linear trend and a yearly wave to its own ten-year series and keep what is left. The trend absorbs the sinking and the wave absorbs most of the thermal expansion; neither is assumed, both are estimated per point. The height gradient of the remainder, and its change from one pass to the next, is the candidate air signal of Findings A. Two things this does not yet do: model the buildings individually (rate and seasonal term per building, with the sideways term of figure 23), and account for a reference point whose own motion is unknown.

Four tests were agreed at the start as the conditions under which the idea is worth pursuing: a height-dependent signal that clears the noise of the fit; a signal that is scene-wide rather than a few buildings; a separation from thermal expansion; and agreement in size with what a real sounding predicts. The first three are met on this data set; the fourth is met in size and, since figure 28, partly in timing.
