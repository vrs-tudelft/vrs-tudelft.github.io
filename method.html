One radar record, two weather records, one building register, and the chain of reasoning that turns a radar phase into a reading of the air.

The data

The test set covers the Wilhelminapier on the south bank of the Nieuwe Maas in Rotterdam: about 530 by 450 m of quays, a few old warehouses, and a row of towers built between 2000 and 2017, among them De Rotterdam (149 m) and the Maastoren (165 m). It was chosen because it has high-rise. It is a best case for the method, not a city average.

TerraSAR-X passed over it 288 times between 10 January 2014 and 30 December 2023, on a descending track, looking to the west-north-west at 23° from vertical. The interval is 11 days for 89 % of the steps, the largest gap is 44 days, and every season is covered about equally. The hour of the pass is not in the file; from the dawn-dusk orbit it should be early morning, but that is our arithmetic, not a confirmed value.

The file holds 20 594 persistent scatterers, reflectors that returned a stable echo on every pass. For each it gives the position, several heights, a coherence (how well the echo fits a stable-point model), a quality number whose definition we do not have, the fitted rate, curvature and yearly wave of its motion, and the 288 displacements themselves in millimetres along the line of sight, all zero on the first pass. Most of the analysis uses the 11 818 points with coherence above 0.6. Two conventions run through the site and neither is confirmed by the data's producer: a positive displacement is read as towards the satellite, so a sinking building has a negative rate; and 10 January 2014 is the zero of every series, whether or not it is also the master image of the processing. There is also a reference point, one reflector against which every displacement is measured, and we do not know where it is, how high, or what it stands on.

Three heights

Three different things get called height, and mixing them is the classic error. The terrain: the file's ground model minus the geoid comes out at 3.0 to 3.5 m above sea level for every point, under the towers as much as on the quays, so it is a terrain model and heights above it are safe. The roofs: the national 3D building register gives one height per building, 46 buildings inside the scene box, the tallest 158 m. The radar points: median 14.7 m above ground, half of them within 31 m of each other, five times more spread than the roofs because a facade returns echoes at many levels. The last one is the height the method depends on.

No radar point is higher than 83 m, although the register confirms four buildings above 100 m in the scene. Three candidate causes, none confirmed: a height search window in the processing; layover, where the top of a tall building is imaged at the same range as ground far in front of it; or upper facades with no surface facing the satellite steadily. The answer decides whether the ceiling of the method in this city is 83 m or 150 m.

The ground truth

Two weather records check the radar against the air. The De Bilt soundings (4 318 in the public archive, 2014 to 2023): launched at midnight 50 km away, with a first archived reading typically 650 m above the ground, so their value for our lowest 83 m is an interpolation. Over that layer they say the refractivity falls by 3.4 N-units on average, 2.3 of which is the predictable pressure part and 1.2 the wet part that varies. Their 11-day change of the layer delay, 1.18 mm per 100 m, is the reference size for what the radar should see. And KNMI Rotterdam, daily temperature and humidity, the stand-in for the temperature of the buildings, lagged by five days. The one fixed alternative to the balloon, the Cabauw tower with instruments at seven levels up to 200 m, also stands in the countryside. The KNMI high-resolution sounding files, with a reading every few metres, exist and are the next thing to get.

The method

A radar signal is delayed by the air it crosses, and the delay is set by the refractivity of that air: the refractive index minus one, times a million, a number around 300 near the ground. Refractivity follows from what a balloon measures, with pressure and vapour pressure in hectopascal and temperature in kelvin:

N = 77.6 pd/T + 72 e/T + 3.75·105 e/T2

The first term is the dry part, predictable from pressure; the last two are the wet part, where the variation is. The delay along the ray is the integral of N, the phase follows from the wavelength, and the slant path from the incidence angle:

d = 10−6 ∫ N ds    ψ = 4π d / λ    slant = zenith / cos 23°

A hundred metres of ordinary air near the ground delays the signal by 31 mm; the whole atmosphere, straight up, by about 2.3 m of which less than 0.3 m is water vapour. Interferometry sees none of those totals, only how they differ between two points and between two passes.

Nothing in interferometry is absolute. Every phase is relative to a reference point in space and to a reference pass in time. Taking a high point minus a low point removes the reference point, the orbit and the air above both; taking this pass minus the first removes the topography and any constant. What survives is the change of the delay through the layer between the two heights, plus the change of the buildings themselves. The state of the first pass can be recovered from the stack afterwards, because the sum of all interferograms equals the sum of the true states minus the first state taken as many times, and the true states average to a known climatology.

With height levels at fixed steps and one refractivity per layer, the delay difference between a level and the reference level is the sum of the layers the ray crosses, so the system is lower triangular and solves from the bottom up, one new unknown per row. In the data so far the profile is a straight line with a fixed kink, so the honest product today is one layer mean per pass. Refractivity per layer is the paper's success criterion; splitting it into a dry and a wet part is the second step; pressure, temperature and humidity separately is the stretch goal and needs one more constraint, for example a surface temperature.

The residuals are made in the simplest defensible way: an offset, a trend and a yearly wave are fitted to every point's own ten-year series and what is left is kept. The trend absorbs the sinking and the wave most of the thermal expansion; neither is assumed, both are estimated per point. Two things this does not yet do: model the buildings individually, with the sideways term the findings found, and account for a reference point whose own motion is unknown. Before the inverse is trusted, the forward model will be run on known inputs: the real heights of the scene, simulated profiles of pressure, temperature and humidity, and the nuisances the real data has, noise, displacement, the cross-range position of a reflector, thermal expansion. That simulation has not been run yet.

The manuscript's abstract speaks of 450 profiles between 2014 and 2024. Our record is 288 passes between 2014 and 2023, on one track. Whether the difference is the ascending track still to come is one of the open questions.

What is still open

Status as of 16 September 2026. The first group can only be settled by the producer of the point set.

  • Why does no radar point sit above 83 m? Roof heights reach 158 m in the scene; every tower above 83 m stops at 57 to 69 m of points. Processing window, layover, or facade orientation.
  • Which height datum, and which sign convention? The terrain check makes "above ground" safe; positive as towards the satellite is the site's reading and would be self-consistent the other way round.
  • Where is the reference point, how high, and on what does it stand? It decides how much of the ground's winter cycle is a mirror image and how much near-surface refractivity the ground points see.
  • Is 10 January 2014 the master image? All series are zero on that date; whether it was the master, and its weather, matters for the stratification signal.
  • What is the local hour of the pass, and does the ascending track exist for this scene? The hour decides which weather data can be compared; a second track would flip the horizontal terms and separate several puzzles.
  • Was an atmospheric filter applied in the processing, and what does the quality column mean? A filter would explain part of why both fitted coefficients are a third short of prediction.
  • Why are both coefficients 60 to 70 % of physics? Buildings not free to expand fits the temperature term (sideways expansion gives 70 % of free); the balloon 50 km away, the pass hour, or a filter could explain the rest.
  • Why does the open ground move in winter? Reference point, near-surface refractivity cycle, or real motion of the fill; the ascending track and the reference point would settle it.
  • What is the kink at 20 to 30 m in the binned profile? Air, or the podiums and setbacks that cluster at those heights.
  • Which temperature does a building feel? A five-day lag of the daily mean fits best, and the buildings follow about half of the air's yearly swing.
  • Can the KNMI high-resolution soundings be obtained? Only they can validate a profile shape below 650 m.
  • What would a boundary-layer meteorologist want from this? Which questions about the lowest hundred metres matter most; what height range, spacing, frequency and accuracy would help; what independent observation would make a layer-average refractivity change useful. Not yet asked.

Words

InSAR
Interferometric synthetic aperture radar: comparing the phase of radar echoes from many passes gives distance changes to a fraction of the 3.1 cm wavelength.
Scatterer, radar point
A piece of a building or pavement that returned a stable echo on every pass.
Pass
One satellite acquisition; 288 of them, every 11 days.
Line of sight
The direction from the satellite to the point, 23° from vertical here. All displacements are measured along it; blue in the maps is away from the satellite.
Above ground, above NAP
Two height systems: metres above the local ground, and metres above the Dutch sea-level datum. The plots use the first unless stated.
Coherence
How well a point's echoes fit a stable-point model, 0 to 1.
Refractivity, N
The refractive index of air minus one, times a million. One number for pressure, temperature and humidity together; humidity dominates the changes.
Height gradient, s(t)
For one pass, the slope of displacement against height across all points, in mm per 100 m. The central quantity of the project.
Trend, yearly wave, fast signal
The three parts of s(t): the sinking of the north-east towers, the thermal expansion of the buildings, and what changes from pass to pass after both are removed.
Double difference
Two points minus each other, then two passes minus each other. What the points share and what does not change cancels.
Reference point, master image
The one reflector and the one pass everything else is measured against in the radar processing. Both unknown to us.
Layover
The top of a tall building imaged at the same range as ground far in front of it. One candidate reason nothing shows above 83 m.
Atmospheric filter
A processing step that removes a smooth atmospheric term from every point. Unknown whether it was applied to our data.
Ascending, descending
The satellite's two directions of travel, looking from opposite sides. We have the descending track, from the east.
BAG, 3DBAG
The Dutch building register with outlines and construction years, and its 3D version with roof heights. Both open data.

What is on this site, and what is not

The radar point set was provided for the course and may not be passed on. Nothing on this site is a radar point. The animations and the 3D view are drawn from small tables of averages: 25 m cells on a fixed grid, buildings, 10 m slabs of buildings, height bands and the whole scene, each a mean over at least 20 points. The positions that appear are those of public things, building outlines from the register and cells on a fixed grid. The script that writes these tables refuses anything smaller, and a second script in the site repository checks every file before it is published. The figures are images; the paper's draft text is not here.
