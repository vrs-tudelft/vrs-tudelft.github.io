Virtual Radiosonde

Kop van Zuid in three dimensions experimental

The buildings of the pier from the open 3D building register, coloured by what the radar saw on them over ten years: how fast they sink, how they tilt, how much they breathe with the seasons and when, and how the ground around them moves, with a time slider over the 288 passes.

Every colour is an aggregate: a mean over at least 20 radar points on one building, in one 10 m slab of a building, or in one 25 m ground cell. No individual measurement point is in this view. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom, right-drag to pan; click a building or a cell for its numbers.

What we can see here that the flat plots do not show

  1. Where the sinking is. Colour by long-term rate: the towers at the north-east end are deep blue, the south-west tip around Hotel New York almost white. Switch to the top-down view and the gradient runs the length of the pier.
  2. Newer is faster. Switch between construction year and rate: the orderings match (2000, 2005, 2010, 2013, 2017 from slow to fast). Click the 1900 hotel: no curvature, still moving slowly.
  3. The 83 m ceiling. Colour by slab and switch on the ceiling plane: every tower goes grey above it although the register's geometry continues to 150 m and more. From the street view the cut is at the same height on every tower.
  4. Only the sides that face the radar carry points. The satellite view with radar lighting: the bright faces are the ones the satellite illuminates from the east-south-east; the dark faces have no data.
  5. The seasonal swing grows with height. Seasonal swing per slab: each tower's colour deepens upward, smoothly, at about 0.03 mm per metre.
  6. Ground and buildings breathe in opposite phase. Buildings peak around day 205 to 215; the open quays, on the cell layer "peak day", around day 45; the cells at the towers' feet are pale on the swing layer.
  7. Sideways expansion. All coloured buildings sit on the positive side, close to the expected +0.015 mm per metre of easting; the wide east-west buildings show it best from above.
  8. What one pass looks like. Play the slider: most passes are pale over the whole pier; some turn the whole scene red or blue at once, never one building alone. The strip chart spikes on the same passes.
  9. The layer of air is thin and tilted. The radar rays: the extra path of the lower ray is only about 80 m of air, at 23° from vertical; the overlay says why 16 N-units make about a millimetre.
  10. A balloon from the data. Launch it on a summer and on a winter pass: the profile leans the opposite way, and the slope matches the marker on the strip chart.
  11. Layover, seen from the satellite. In the satellite view the tall towers cover the ground behind them towards the west-north-west; the radar cannot see there either, which is why there are no cells at the foot of the towers on that side.
