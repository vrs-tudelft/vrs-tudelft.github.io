Virtual Radiosonde

Buildings: sinking, shrinking and growing in the heat

The radar points sit on buildings, and buildings move. Before the radar can say anything about the air, the movement of every building has to be modelled and taken out. This page is chapter 4 of the report: the model, one term at a time, and what the radar record of Kop van Zuid looks like.

Why the buildings matter

The project reads the air from how much more the high radar points seem to move than the low ones. The air's share of that is tiny: one N-unit of refractivity across 100 m of height changes the radar distance by about a tenth of a millimetre.

The buildings move far more. A 100 m tower grows by about 1 mm for every degree it warms, while that degree changes the air's delay across the same 100 m by only about 0.12 mm. Over a year, the towers of Kop van Zuid swing by millimetres with the seasons, and over the years they settle by several millimetres more. That is ten to a few hundred times the air signal, and it also grows with height. The buildings therefore have to be modelled first, and modelled well.

The report models a building's movement as three processes added together:

  • Sinking

    The whole building goes down at a steady rate. The same at every height.

  • Shrinking

    The building slowly shortens under its own weight, faster at first. Grows with height.

  • Thermal dilation

    Taller when warm, shorter when cold. Grows with height and follows the seasons.

What is measured

Take one building with m radar points spread over its height, seen on n passes. For point i on pass j, the radar gives yij, its movement along the radar's line of sight since a first pass t0. The point sits at height hi above the foundation and the radar looks at it at an angle θi from the vertical, about 23° here. If the building only moves up and down, the radar sees the part of that movement along its line of sight:

pi = cos θi,  Δtj = tj − t0,  ΔTj = T(tj) − T(t0)report eqs. 40 and 41

A positive movement is towards the satellite, so up. That is also the sign of the radar data we use: four independent checks agree on it (Estimation). Time and temperature are counted from the first pass, so every term below is zero on that pass.

In the sliders below, two points on one building are followed: one 85 m up and one 5 m up, 80 m apart like the estimation's two 40 m layers. The temperature is the real daily mean at Rotterdam airport on each of the 213 pass dates from 2020 to 2026. The red line, high minus low, is the height contrast: the part that looks like air and that the estimation has to remove.

Sinking

dsink(tj) = vs Δtjreport eq. 42

The whole building moves down as one block, at a constant speed vs. The foundation and the roof go down together, so sinking does not depend on height or on temperature. On its own it changes nothing in the height contrast: try it.

Shrinking

A building under its own load slowly gets shorter: concrete creeps and shrinks. The shortening is fastest just after construction and slows down over the years. Because the radar only measures from t0 onwards, the moment construction ended does not need to be known. The model only needs how fast the building was shortening on the first pass, γ0, and how quickly that slows down, the relaxation time τc:

dεshrink/dt = −γ0 exp(−(t − t0) / τc),  γ0 ≥ 0, τc > 0report eq. 43, the shortening rate

Adding this up from the first pass, and assuming the whole height shortens by the same fraction, a point at height hi comes down by

dshrink(hi, tj) = −hi γ0 τc [1 − exp(−Δtj / τc)]report eqs. 44 and 45

So the higher the point, the more it comes down, and the rate fades with time. With a long τc the curve is almost a straight line, indistinguishable from a steady trend; with a short one it bends and levels off within the record.

Thermal dilation

dthermal(hi, tj) = hi α ΔTjreport eq. 46

A building grows when it warms and shrinks when it cools, by the same fraction α per degree over its whole height. For concrete, α is about 10 × 10⁻⁶ per kelvin (Eurocode 2): a 100 m tower that warms by 1 K grows by 1 mm. The temperature is counted from the first pass, so the term is zero there.

This is the hard term. The air also changes with temperature, and its effect also grows with height. Seen from the satellite, a warm building and warm air can look the same. How the estimation keeps them apart is under Parameter estimation.

The combined model

The movement of point i on pass j is the sum of the three:

dij(x) = vs Δtj − hi γ0 τc [1 − exp(−Δtj / τc)] + hi α ΔTjreport eq. 47
x = [vs γ0 τc α]T,  yij = pi dij(x) + εijreport eqs. 48 and 49

Four numbers per building. Everything the model does not explain ends up in εij: the noise, and the air we are after. Since all three terms are zero on the first pass, no extra offset is needed. Taking it apart: trend and yearly wave

One number per pass for the whole scene, the height gradient s(t): how much more the high points moved than the low ones, in mm per 100 m of height. It is the height contrast of the sliders above, measured. The player takes it apart in four steps: as measured; the slow trend removed (shrinking, and towers settling faster than the ground), which in this set is small (-0.03 mm per 100 m per year), the north-east towers sinking faster than the ground; the yearly wave removed (thermal dilation, the buildings growing in summer); and the remainder differenced from pass to pass. That leftover, about 1.6 mm per 100 m, is 7 times the formal noise of the fit and about 15 N-units if it were all air. That does not make it air: a fit for the buildings can also absorb air that follows the temperature, which is why the estimation uses an air prior.

Parameter estimation

All observations of one building go into one vector y = f(x) + ε, and the four parameters are found by weighted least squares, with γ0 ≥ 0 and τc > 0 (report eqs. 50 and 51). The model is nonlinear only in τc. For a fixed τc it is linear in vs, γ0 and α, so those three follow from ordinary weighted least squares, and τc is the value that leaves the smallest weighted residual (eqs. 52 to 56). The team's code searches τc from 0.25 years upwards on a logarithmic grid and then refines it.

gj(τc) = τc [1 − exp(−Δtj / τc)],  yij = pi [Δtj −higj hiΔTj] [vs γ0 α]T + εijreport eqs. 52 to 55

One problem the report's model cannot solve by itself: the thermal term hi α ΔTj and an air change that follows the temperature are both proportional to height, so for one building they cannot be told apart. Fitted naively, α swallows part of the air. The estimation therefore first takes out what the weather station predicts for the air, fits the buildings, and puts the prediction back afterwards: the air prior. On the simulation that gets α back to 7.02 × 10⁻⁶ /K against a true 7.00, where the naive fit gives 9.05.

What is left: the residuals

êij = yij − ŷij,  RMSe = √( Σ êij2 / N )report eqs. 57 to 60

With the four parameters estimated, the model's movement of every point is subtracted from what the radar measured. What is left, the residual, is read as the air plus noise. It can also hold anything the model leaves out: local deformation of one facade, a building that does not behave like a single block, or measurement errors. The root mean square of the residuals measures how much is left. Plotted against time, height and temperature, the residuals show where the model falls short; a pattern there means a process the four parameters do not describe.

The residuals are averaged per height layer and per pass and turned into refractivity. The report compares motion fitted per building or per scatterer; ground-station agreement does not independently validate the vertical profile.
