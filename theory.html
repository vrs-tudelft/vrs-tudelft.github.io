Theory: how the air shows up in a radar measurement

Radar waves travel a little slower through air than through empty space, and moist air slows them more than dry air. This page follows the physics from the radar's phase to the refractivity of the air, and states what a double difference between buildings and passes can and cannot tell us.

Status on 13 October 2026. Condensed from chapter 2 (Theory) of the team's report draft, version 0.1.

In short

  • Phase is distance. Comparing a high and a low point on buildings, and one pass with another, leaves mostly the change in delay through the air between the two heights. Below
  • The delay is the refractivity N, summed along the path. N has a large dry part set by pressure and temperature and a smaller wet part set by water vapour. Try the calculator
  • Water vapour matters most. One hPa of vapour changes N as much as nearly four degrees of temperature. Whether warming raises or lowers the delay depends on what the humidity does at the same time. Below
  • The radar sees changes, never the absolute profile. Pressure can be predicted almost exactly; water vapour is the real unknown; anything common to both passes cancels. Below

From phase to delay

A SAR image stores, for every resolution cell, an amplitude and a phase. That phase is the sum of three parts: the distance from antenna to target, the delay of the wave in the air, and a random part from the many small reflectors inside the cell.

Two images taken from almost the same orbit, days apart, are combined into an interferogram. If the target is stable, such as a building corner, the random part is the same in both images and cancels. The two orbits are not exactly the same, so a height-dependent pattern remains; it is computed from an elevation model (AHN5) and subtracted. After unwrapping, what is left is a change in delay, plus any real movement of the target.

d = − (λ / 4π) · φ,   λ ≈ 3.1 cm, so one full phase cycle = 1.55 cm of one-way path

That is why the radar resolves millimetres: it measures a fraction of a 1.55 cm cycle, not a travel time. The radar looks about 23° from vertical; a simple cosine converts between the slant path and the vertical, and is accurate enough at such a steep angle.

The double difference

A single delay contains the whole atmosphere, kilometres of it. The trick of this project is to subtract twice. Between points: the ray to a low point B crosses one extra layer of air that the ray to a high point A does not; everything above A is common and cancels. Between passes: a later pass (a daughter) is compared with the first one (the mother), so whatever did not change cancels too.

Refractivity of moist air

How much the air slows the wave is its refractive index n, about 1.0003 near the ground. Because only the fourth decimal matters, we use the refractivity N = (n − 1) × 10⁶, a number of a few hundred.

Two mechanisms make up N at radar frequencies. The dry (or hydrostatic) term: the wave's field nudges the electrons of nitrogen and oxygen molecules; it depends only on how many molecules there are, so on pressure and temperature. The wet term: water molecules are permanent dipoles that partly turn with the field, which adds a contribution that grows with the water vapour and falls steeply with temperature. Free electrons in the ionosphere and cloud droplets add smaller effects that are left out here.

N = k₁ · Pd / T  +  k₂ · e / T  +  k₃ · e / T²

with Pd the pressure of the dry air and e the partial pressure of water vapour, both in hPa, T in kelvin, and k₁ = 77.6 K/hPa, k₂ = 72 K/hPa, k₃ = 3.75 × 10⁵ K²/hPa (Smith and Weintraub, 1953). The first term is the dry part, the last two together the wet part. The delay is N summed along the path:

d = 10⁻⁶ ∫ N ds

Sizes: large is not the same as variable

Expressed as a delay straight up through the whole atmosphere, the four contributions differ a lot in size:

≈ 2.3 mdry, hydrostatic: by far the largest
< 0.3 mwet: water vapour
mmcloud liquid water, even under thick cloud
smallionosphere, at X band (far larger at L band)

Interferometry, however, only sees what changes between passes and between points. The dry term is large but smooth and slow; the wet term is smaller but varies much more in space and time, and that is what the radar picks up.

The Saastamoinen model

The standard way to predict the total delay straight up, the zenith total delay, from weather at the ground alone:

ZTD = ZHD + ZWD
ZHD = 0.0022768 · P / (1 − 0.00266 cos 2φ − 0.00028 H)
ZWD = 0.002277 · (1255 / T + 0.05) · e

with P and e at the surface in hPa, T in kelvin, latitude φ and station height H in km; the result is in metres. For Rotterdam (φ ≈ 52°, H ≈ 0) and the air set in the calculator: ZHD = 2.31 m, ZWD = 0.10 m, total 2.41 m, or 2.62 m along a slant path at 23°.

Saastamoinen does not retrieve a profile. It turns three surface numbers into a column total by assuming how the atmosphere is built above the station. A mapping function then converts the zenith value to the radar's slant path.

Temperature: two effects, one sign problem

When the air between two passes is warmer, two things happen at once to a high point on a building, compared with a point at the street:

  1. The building expands. Concrete grows by about 10 × 10⁻⁶ per kelvin, so a point 100 m up rises about 1 mm per K.
  2. The air changes its refractivity, which changes the delay on the ray to the low point. Its size is only about a tenth of the expansion, and its sign depends on the humidity.

Dry case: vapour pressure stays the same

Hold P and e fixed and warm the air by 1 K, from 15 to 16 °C at 50 % humidity. N drops by about 1.2. Over 100 m that is 0.12 mm less delay: the low point seems to come up, so the high point seems to go down. This works against the expansion: 1 − 0.12 = 0.88 mm net.

Wet case: relative humidity stays the same

Over days to weeks the vapour pressure rarely stays put. If instead the relative humidity is held at 50 %, the warmer air carries more vapour, because the saturation vapour pressure rises steeply with temperature: e goes from 8.53 to 9.07 hPa. Now N rises by about 1.2 to 1.3: the same size, opposite sign. The air effect reinforces the expansion: 1 + 0.12 = 1.12 mm.

The real, hybrid case

In reality neither e nor the relative humidity stays fixed. Ten years of hourly observations at KNMI station 344, Rotterdam airport, show that temperature explains most of the vapour pressure (e = 0.511 T + 5.262 hPa, R² = 0.715) but little of the relative humidity (R² = 0.272). Most hours fall on the wet side of the boundary below, where warming raises N; a real minority, mostly cold and dry hours, falls on the dry side.

The practical consequence: the sign and size of the air's contribution cannot be read from the temperature change alone. Separating expansion from air needs the vapour pressure at each pass, from temperature with humidity or dew point. That is the job of the ground weather station in this project.

Concepts: what has to be assumed

One number, a whole profile

The radar gives one number per point and pass: a delay summed along the path, already a double difference. We want a profile, N(z), or better P(z), T(z) and e(z). That is an inverse problem, and under-determined: one integral fits infinitely many profiles. Every method of this kind, GNSS meteorology and delay models for geodesy included, closes the gap with assumptions about how the atmosphere is built. The question is which assumptions are cheap and which are expensive.

Pressure: nearly free

Pressure follows hydrostatic balance: it falls exponentially with a scale height of about 8.4 km. Over the lowest 150 m that is almost a straight line, about 1 hPa per 8 to 9 m, whatever the weather. With the calculator's surface pressure: 1008 hPa at 40 m NAP and 995 hPa at 150 m NAP, which by itself lowers N by about 3.7 per 100 m. So P(z) is fixed from one ground observation at almost no cost; this is also why Saastamoinen's hydrostatic delay is accurate to millimetres.

Temperature and moisture: the real unknown

Water vapour is not layered so neatly. It is carried, mixed and trapped by turbulence near the ground, and in cities by the layering above roofs and streets. The dry and wet cases above predict opposite signs for the same warming, and Rotterdam's hours fall on both sides. So e(z), or T(z) and humidity together, is the genuinely unresolved part, and it is exactly the part a fixed climatological model cannot supply case by case.

Is a ground station enough?

Saastamoinen and similar surface models map one set of ground values onto the column total, with an assumed decay of N with height fitted to years of balloon soundings. For the dry part this works almost by definition. For the wet part it works only on average: it cannot see tonight's shallow, humid inversion or today's well-mixed layer. A station constrains the total well, but not the vertical distribution. Radar points at many heights on many buildings can add that, not by replacing the station but on top of it, with the station as the starting point and prior.

The common-mode limitation

Every radar observation is a difference in time and in space. Anything in the refractivity that is the same at both passes cancels and can never be recovered, however good the data. The method can only retrieve changes of the vertical structure between passes, or, after stacking on one mother, relative to an estimated mean; never an absolute profile.
